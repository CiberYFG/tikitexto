What the lesson explains

Today we're looking at the most famous and most debated rule in football: offside. You'll see it's easier than it looks.

We have an attacking team in blue and a defending team in red. At the back is the goalkeeper, who is almost always the last opponent.

The second-last opponent is usually the last outfield defender. Imagine a line through him, parallel to the goal line: that is the offside line.

A forward is in an offside position if, when the ball is played to him, he is closer to the opponent's goal than both the ball and the second-last opponent. This forward has gone past the line: offside.

If the forward is level with the defender, right on the line, he is onside. Being level is legal.

And if you stay behind the line, of course, you're onside. This forward can receive the ball with no problem.

Careful: what matters is where you are at the moment of the pass, not where you end up. If you were level when the ball was played and then run towards goal, it's not offside.

There is also no offside if you receive the ball directly from a throw-in, a corner kick or a goal kick. And it can never happen in your own half.

Also, being in an offside position is not an offence by itself: the referee only punishes it if that player gets involved in play, by touching the ball or interfering with an opponent. The penalty is an indirect free kick for the other team.

Today the VAR and virtual lines also help measure the tightest calls. Now you know the basics. Next time you see it in a match, you'll understand it right away!
